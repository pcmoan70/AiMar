Summary/Recommendation:

The aquaculture production of Atlantic salmon in Norway is diversifying and many different production systems,
both at sea and on-land are currently being tested and evaluated. One of these systems is the new laminar flow
production pool developed by Andfjord Salmon AS. Fish production in the patented Andfjord salmon flow through
pool system. The system supplies a continuous flow of 100% seawater to the salmon, pumped from 45 m deep.
20 000 m3 of seawater are pumped into the pool per hour, ca. 333 m3/minute and estimated water exchange in
the pool is 15-17 times per 24-hour period. The rearing pool is 42 m long x 40 m wide with a mean water depth of
ca. 17.5 m deep. Water volume is ca. 29 400 + 1500 m>. The production pool utilises laminar water flow via a
pressure drop wall at the entrance to the pool. This leads to a turbulence free water flow through the pool and
sediment falls to the pool substrate.

Production in the pool is currently underway and salmon have been stocked in the system since 25 June 2022.
Andfjord Salmon have drawn up a very comprehensive welfare and health documentation plan for the first
production cycle. This mid-way report documents the welfare and health of the fish for the first ca. six months of
stocking in the system until 20 December 2022, using a suite of input and outcome-based welfare indicators, in
addition to a number of additional health indicators.

A wide range of environmental OWlIs have documented mostly uniform high levels of key water quality parameters
such as oxygen throughout the pool. Water current speeds, whilst low, have not manifested in poor water quality
and seems to be increased by fish presence in the system. Minor differences in measured water quality
parameters were found at different locations in the system but should not have consequence for fish welfare and
should allow for uniform distribution of fish in the system so far. As water is drawn from depth before being pumped
into the system, water temperature is somewhat more stable than what would be expected at corresponding
depths in open cages and is following seasonal patterns in ambient water temperature. Although the oxygen has
been added to the inlet water the effect of water temperature and increased biomass on dissolved oxygen
saturation was observed during most intensive parts of the production. The low natural water temperatures the
fish have been subjected to over the autumn/early winter have currently not manifested in any welfare or
performance risks, or risks for e.g., winter ulcers.

Mortalities have been very low in the rearing system (ca. 1.6% for the entire documentation period) and any minor
mortality events are closely monitored and remedial actions, where possible to identify and apply, have been
taken. Appetite has generally been good in the first six months of the project and growth performance and
condition factor have been good. Fish have adapted to the rearing system and are generally utilising the pool in
a uniform manner. No fleeing/panic behaviours have been observed at observation times around high and low
water, and where potential fish sensitivity to the ROV lighting in late October led to short-term panic in some fish
and a short-term increase in associated mortalities, remedial action was taken to ensure this unfortunate
occurrence was short-lived and corrected for.

The injury status of fish in the system was closely monitored during monthly manual audits of high numbers of
fish in relation to the Laksvel OWI scoring system (Nilsson et al., 2022). The injury status of the fish has generally
been good with the majority of injury based OWls were scored as absent or mild. When OWI levels increase this
was noted and causes investigated. After smolt transfer there were a number of fish with poor condition
(emaciation state) and scale loss (< 20% of fish sampled) but this injury status improved 90 days after stocking.
Monthly samples were taken from kidney, skin, and gills for histological examination to evaluate the integrity and
quality of these organs. Gill health was generally good at all timepoints, whereas skin got a higher score (more
damaged) from the hatchery phase up until 60 days post-transfer to the pool. This skin health score is higher than
what is expected from fish sampled during similar stages in the production cycle. Histological findings in skin were
comparable to the welfare score and included superficial damages such as lost epidermis and scale loss over the
same time points, in the majority of analysed individuals. Based on these findings, skin morphology and skin
health will be followed closer in the coming months. Fish developed Nephrocalcinosis in the hatchery phase before
transfer to the Andfjord pool and we see the artefacts of this after their transfer to seawater. With regard to
pathogen screening, fish from TO-T5 were all categorized with suspected heart- and skeletal muscle inflammation
(HSMI), with positive results for Piscine orthoreovirus 1 (PRV-1). At T4 and T5, 3% of sampled fish was positive
for the parasite Paranucleospora theridion. There were negative results for all other pathogen screening.

Whilst only a negligible amount of Lepeophtheirus salmonis has been observed in the system, there is a noticeable
but relatively low abundance of Caligus elongatus. For now, this does not pose a major risk to fish health or
welfare and can easily be remedied by feed administered treatments. Infestation levels should nevertheless be
monitored closely for the remainder of the production cycle.

3.3 Fish stocking and history

Fish were stocked in the Andfjord production pool KO on the 25! of June 2022. Fish were produced by
Nordland Akva AS, Åmøya locality (locality number: 26375) and were from a pooled hatchery Saga
stock. Fish were collected from the hatchery on 24 of June 2022 by the Bjørg Pauline wellboat. Water
temperature salinity and dissolved oxygen (DO) levels in the hatchery at time of transfer were 8.0 °C,
12 ppm and 95.8 %, respectively. Fish were transferred in water with a temperature range of 8.5 — 10.5
°C. Water temperature and DO levels in the Andfjord KO pool at time of transfer were ca. 7.5 °C and
108 % at 7 m, respectively. Average weight of fish at time of stocking was ca. 120 g and 198 883 fish
were transferred into the pool.

3.4 Characteristics of the rearing system

A detailed outline of the rearing pool design and system characteristics can be found in the Andfjord
Salmon Biosikkerhetsplan drawn up by Akerbla AS (and revised on 15.12.2022)

In brief, the Andfjord Salmon land-based production pool KO is flow-through, and it supplies a continuous
flow of 100% seawater to the salmon, pumped from 40 m deep. 20 000 m? of seawater are pumped
into the pool per hour, ca. 333 m?/minute and estimated water exchange in the pool is 15-17 times per
24-hour period. The KO pool is sunken into a concrete covered outer pool and consists of an internal
composite tank/pool that is fixed to the substrate of the outer pool and has four walls. The rearing pool
is 42 m long x 40 m wide with a mean water depth of ca. 17.5 m deep. Water volume is ca. 29 400 +
1500 m3. The KO production pool utilises laminar water flow via a pressure drop wall at the entrance to
the pool. This leads to a turbulence free water flow through the pool and sediment falls to the pool
substrate (see section 3.7). For further details of the pool and system design see Figures 3 — 5 below.

3.9 Fish welfare documentation: input-based water velocity and water quality
OWls

One of the main aims of this report was to document input based OWls, specifically water quality
including water velocity and light conditions in the pool. This documentation has taken two forms: i)
Nofima has carried out vertical and horizontal profiling of various water quality parameters on three
occasions in 2022, while Andfjord Salmon have also monitored number of water quality parameters in
real time and made this data available for documentation and analysis by Nofima.

3.9.1 Vertical and horizontal profiling of water velocity and water quality in the system
Nofima has done three profiles of the system in the midway reporting period, one without fish (before
smolt transfer on the 8 and 9' June 2022, Figure 2) and two with fish in the system (on the 14" and
15th June 2022, without underwater lights and on the 13 and 14 December 2022, with underwater
lights). For the purpose of this report only the results from the first two profiles will be included.

å

Figure 7: System water quality and water velocity profiling carried out by Nofima in June 2022.

In order to document water quality and velocity in the system, a total of 20 sampling points were audited
3 m from the pool inlet and 3 m from the pool outlet within the pilot pool system (see Figure 8). These
transects specifically audited water parameters at five horizonal points: 1, 10, 20, 30 and 39 m from one
of the system walls and four vertical points at 1, 5, 10 and 15 m depth. At those points the following
parameters were measured: oxygen, temperature, pH, salinity, turbidity and water velocity (Vector water
velocity meter, Rud, Norway). All probes for water quality measurements were connected to the hand-
held instrument (WTW multi 3630 IDS) and measurements were recorded for further analysis. As water
velocity measurements were deemed to be particularly important due to the system design and
anticipated low water flows in the pool (due to concerns from the Norwegian Food Safety Authority
regarding potential effects of low water current speed upon fish welfare, see the Norwegian Food Safety
Authority’s criteria for establishing the production system letter ref: 2017/077177), An additional 12
points, three horizonal and four vertical (Figure 8), were chosen in the middle of the system for more in
depth documentation of water velocity.

Measurement at the system inlet and in the middle of the pool were carried out on day one and at the
system outlet on day two of sampling, making sure that all measurements were done at similar times in

3.9.2 Real-time monitoring of water quality in the system

water leaving the system. Additional sensors were also placed in the inlet and outlet chambers as
indicated in Figure 9. Horizontal differences in the system were accounted for by multiple sensors placed
at different locations. All sensors were integrated into the Clarify platform (Searis, Norway) and values
were continuously monitored and logged. Nofima was given access to all measurements for
documentation purposes.

3.10 Fish welfare documentation — group and individual outcome based OWls

Fish welfare in the Andfjord KO pool is also being documented with a range of outcome based OWls at
both the group and individual level.

3.10.1 Group level Outcome based OWls

Mortalities are collected and recorded daily by Andfjord Salmon staff. These are further grouped
according to suspected cause of death in accordance with the scheme from Aunsmo et al., (2023). We
retrieved the daily and weekly total mortality numbers from June 25" until December 20" 2022 and
calculated the mortality rates for a given day or, week, or month as the number of mortalities on that
day, week, or month divided by the incoming stock on that day, week, or month. The daily and weekly
rates were presented as per-mille (%0) and the monthly rates as percentages (%). The mortality rates
were compared against the mortality rate scoring scheme from Laksvel (Table 3) (Nilsson et al., 2022).
Cumulative mortalities were calculated as the sum of daily mortalities through the period and the period
mortality rate as the sum of daily mortalities through the period divided by the estimated incoming stock

3.10.2. Individual level Outcome based OWls

Morphological OWls have been recorded ca. monthly since smolt transfer on ca. 100 individuals
according to the Laksvel OWI scoring protocol for Atlantic salmon (see Nilsson et al., 2022). Laksvel
standardises a range of morphological OWls into a 0 — 3 scoring scheme where: 0 = undamaged, 1 =
minor injury, 2 = clear moderate injury, 3 = severe injury (see Nilsson et al., 2022 for a full overview and
a pictorial scoring guide). Laksvel scores 14 morphological OWls based upon: i) first impression, ii)
vertebral deformities, iii) emaciation state, iv) maturation state, v) scale loss, vi) skin haemorrhaging, vii)
lesions/wounds, viii) snout damage, ix) jaw deformities, x) eye opacity xi) eye damage, xii) opercular
damage, xiii) gill status, xiv) fin damage and all 14 indicators have been included in the Andfjord Salmon

4.3 Fish health monitoring
4.3.1 Skin health histology

It is expected that skin quality is reduced after transfer to sea, and that it improves after 1-2 weeks.
Reduced quality is generally associated with damaged epidermis, missing epidermis, scale loss or
altered mucus production. Alterations in mucus production can be an increased/decreased number of
mucus cells, changed position of the mucus cells in the epidermis, or changes in the composition of
mucus produced by the mucus cells.

Manual scoring was done to evaluate skin features and its quality, and included scoring of the epidermis,
mucus cells, scale loss and melanin. Scoring showed that most of the outer part of the epidermis was
missing in samples from T1, as shown below (Figure 26).

Figure 26: Examples of skin with intact outer epidermis (right) and missing outer epidermis (green) and scales (red
X) (right).

Thirty percent of the T-1 samples from the hatchery prior to pool transfer had a damaged epidermis.
This is higher than what is expected from samples taken from healthy fish of good quality. At TO, the

5 Discussion

As stated earlier in this report, knowledge on how to select and apply appropriate, fit for purpose OWI
and LABWI toolboxes for documenting fish health and welfare in new and emerging aquaculture
systems is currently lacking. An aim of the Andfjord Salmon documentation project was therefore to use
a wide-ranging and comprehensive suite of OWIs/LABWIs to document the possible effects of the
rearing system and its operations upon the welfare and health of the fish it rears.

5.1 Fish welfare documentation
5.1.1 Input-based water velocity and water quality OWls

The Andfjord Salmon system design anticipated low water velocities in the range of ~1cm/s that could
potentially affect swimming behaviour, distribution of the fish and water quality in the system. In order
to address those concerns an extensive profiling of water velocity both vertically and horizontally was
done in the system without and with fish. In the system without fish, average velocity for three profiles
were in the range of expected, ca. 1 cm/s. The lowest average velocities were recorded in the middle of
the system, furthest from the potential positive effect of inlet and outlet formations. The highest velocity
measurements corelated with the location of the two outlets (10 and 30 m from the reference wall) that
have likely created vortexes extending at least 3 m into the system where measurements were done. At
those points, velocity was almost 3 times higher compared to other measurements in the system. Similar
observations are made for large commercial circular and octagonal tanks where peak velocities were
related to central bottom drains in the tanks (Gorle et al, 2018). Interestingly, the water velocity in the
system was not reduced by introduction of fish to the system. On the contrary, average velocities for all
horizontal and vertical profiles increased between 2 and 6 times. For circular and octagonal tanks, a
25% drop in the velocity is suggested due to the presence of fish biomass (Gorle et al., 2018). Again,
the peak velocities were related to the vicinity of system outlet and at bigger depths. There were also
ROV observations during regular cleaning and inspection of the system of fish seeking out potentially
higher water velocities near the tank outlet in the month after pool stocking (C. Thomassen personal
communication, see behavioural section below). One of the potential reasons for different velocities
between two samplings was that during measurements in the system without fish, the pumps were
running at 90% capacity. This could have led to slightly lower measured water velocities. However, it is
very plausible that the fish are responsible for the observed overall increase in water velocity. Fish create
turbulence by their motion and when considering that the first velocity profile with the fish was carried
out in mid-September, almost three months after fish were introduced in the system when their reported
distribution in the system was uniform, this assumption can be feasible.

Dissolved oxygen saturation, when measured at all 20 points within system, with and without fish, was
above 85%. Minor stratification of oxygen was observed close to the outlet in the system without fish,
where the lowest values were recorder closest to the surface and highest at 15 m depth. This could
have been corelated with the temperature stratification at the same locations where the highest
temperatures were measured at 1 m depth. In September, we recorded lower average dissolved oxygen
saturation 3 m from the tank outlet (92.1 %) compared to measurements done the day earlier at the
same time 3 m from the inlet chamber (97.0 %). It is important to say that this was a consequence of
the sharp temperature drop (3 °C difference in average temperature) from one day to the other.
Considering the temperature ranges recorded during profiling and daily averages of real-time
measurements, oxygen values were always in the green zone indicated by Remen et al., (2016) and
Nilsson et al., (2022). The availability of the real-time oxygen measurement allowed for increased
traceability of the environmental conditions fish are exposed to during production. A closer look at the
daily average oxygen saturations showed correlations between inlet values and values recorded in the
system and outlet chamber during first two months of the production. This trend was perfectly correlated
to the increase in temperature that occurred over the same time period. It was also clear that the

The daily feed delivery increased from stocking through the beginning of September and decreased

in daylength significantly influenced the daily feed ration delivered by an on-demand feeding system.
The lower oxygen saturation (as % of air saturation) threshold for maximal feed intake for the relevant
temperature range was observed to be < 55% (Remen et al., 2016), which the oxygen levels in the
Andfjord system exceeds by far throughout the studied period. Whilst artificial lighting is used within the
Andfjord salmon system, feed is generally administered during natural daylight or twilight hours. A short-
term drop was observed following the onset of artificial lighting, and likely also following an incident
where an ROV with mounted lamps scared the fish near the end of October, possibly reflecting a short-

conversion rate observed, the recorded feed delivery should indicate a good appetite throughout —
especially when considering the novelty of the system and its operation.

Behaviour is a valuable, if complex OWI in salmonid aquaculture (Wiese et al., 2023) and how salmon
behaviourally respond to their rearing environment and external stimuli can be an indicator of how they
have adapted to the system they are reared in. A simple behavioural OW] toolbox was developed to
document an array of behavioural OWls, including: group structure; occurrences of clumping
behaviours; the vertical and horizontal distribution and utilisation of the pool, and any occurrences of
fleeing/panic behaviour. Appetite is also a valuable OWI (Stien et al., 2013) and staff impressions of
daily appetite were also scored according to Laksvel (Nilsson et al., 2022) in addition to their impressions
of daily surfacing activity.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that salmon in aquacultural settings distribute themselves in
relation to numerous environmental gradients (see e.g., Oppedal et al., 2011 and references therein). A
major focus of the Andfjord Salmon welfare and health documentation plan has been to document a
wide array of input-based environmental indicators within the pool in order to aid our understanding of
the spatio-temporal effects of these OWls upon the fish and the behaviours they express. As outlined
above, this the environmental OWI! documentation work has demonstrated a somewhat stable spatial
variability in key environmental OWls within the Andfjord pool, where key OWls such as oxygen can be
classified as mostly optimal for fish size, life stage and a given water temperature (see Remen et al.,
2016). Over 60% of daily temperature readings were in the range 10 — 16 °C and this range is considered
to be optimal for sea farmed salmon according to Nilsson et al., (2022). The remainder of the average
daily seawater temperatures were in the range 7 — 10 °C which can increase the risk for welfare issues
such as winter ulcer (Nilsson et al., 2022) but this was not noted during the first 6 months of production.

During initial pool acclimation, farm staff observations noted that fish distributed themselves around the
periphery of the pool and distributed themselves in the upper layers of the water column during working
hours, often seeking shade during the periods of direct sunlight in the first week following smolt transfer.
Fish rapidly adapted to the pool and within weeks their horizontal and vertical distribution at high and
low water observation periods was classified as stable and uniform. After pool acclimation, the fish
generally avoided the periphery of the rearing system unless they were moribund. From early July 2022,
fish exhibited a uniform and stable horizontal pool distribution and this persisted until the end of the
reporting period. Other studies have reported that in open cage systems fish generally avoid the cage
periphery and base (Oppedal et al., 2011) and adopt a somewhat loose schooling group swimming
structure. Loose schooling was the dominant group structure observed during behavioural inspections
at low and high water and accounted for between 80-85% of all observations. The remainder of
observations mostly noted mixed behaviours. Incidences of large-scale uniform schooling behaviour
were rare (2 — 3 incidences) and loose schooling often involved smaller scale sub-groups of fish. This
may be related to the generally low water velocities within the pool, as faster current speeds reportedly

5.2 Fish health monitoring

External fish health services have reported good gill health during the first six months of stocking in the
Andfjord pilot pool. This correlates with the low number of gill health risks the fish have been exposed
to. Various gill health risks can include handling (e.g., mechanical delousing), high turbidity and poor
water quality and fish have not been exposed to these risks in the Andfjord system. Pathogen pressure
is also low (ref: screening results), see Noble et al., (2018), Sommerset et al., (2022) and references
therein.

Nephrocalcinosis is seen as one of the biggest health challenges for salmon in hatcheries, with higher
risk in RAS facilities due to aspects related to water quality (Sommerset et al., 2022). In the fish from
the Andfjord system, the salmon developed Nephrocalcinosis in the hatchery phase before transfer to
the Andfjord pool and we see the artefacts of this after their transfer to seawater.

Histological examination of skin from monthly samples taken during the later stages of the hatchery
phase up until ca. 60 days post seater transfer showed more damaged epidermis than what is normally
observed in similar cases. It is expected that skin quality is reduced after transfer to sea, and that it
improves after 1-2 weeks, but this was not the case. Skin score continued to be high through all sampling
points. These findings were similar to observations of samples analysed by Patogen AS (ref: screening
results), reporting lost epidermis, inflammation and superficial damages in the majority of analysed
individuals. In response to these findings, project partners have increased the frequency of skin health
sampling (in the original documentation plan skin samples were planned to be audited ca. every 3 — 6
months after T2 until slaughter). Interestingly, the amounts of macro-level scale loss (audited as a
Laksvel OWI after Nilsson et al., 2022) were also worst at sampling points T1 - T2 (ca. 30- and 60-days
post-seawater transfer, respectively), before improving in later months (see Figure 25).

5.3 Production performance monitoring

The growth, represented by the specific growth rate (SGR) and thermal growth coefficient (TGC), has
been higher throughout the period than that indicated for fish in this weight and temperature range (4 to
14 °C) by reference growth tables (Austreng et al., 1987). It is also in the upper range of values reported
in several previous studies, overall. For the period in question, especially from late August until October,
the reported values are above the range shown by previous studies (Dessen et al., 2017, Thorarensen
and Farrell, 2011, Mørkøre and Rørvik, 2001). Einen et al. (1995) considered a well performing farmed
Atlantic salmon to have a TGC around 3.3, overall. It is worth noting that previous studies have mostly

43
og

been conducted at more southerly locations, experiencing longer days during the winter months, and in
sea cages, experiencing different water velocities. The growth in the Andfjord salmon system is high
also when compared with studies utilising semi-closed containment systems, although these studies are
not easily comparable due to having been conducted during different seasons or using different
sampling regimes (Nilsen et al., 2019, Balseiro et al., 2018).

The standard deviation of the average weights is increasing slightly throughout the studied period but is
lower than values observed for sea cage rearing in previous studies (e.g., Mørkøre and Rørvik, 2001,
Roth et al., 2005). The values are likely representative of, or lower than, those normally experienced in
fish populations not subjected to periodic weight grading, as in sea cage aquaculture. The condition
factor rose from the start, reaching a peak during the end of September at 1.44, and then declined
sharply towards the end of the year. A similar pattern has previously been observed in cage reared
salmon released as one-year old smolts. Typically, Atlantic salmon in sea cage aquaculture tend to
accumulate fat throughout the autumn (Dessen et al., 2017). This fat accumulation stagnates during the
winter months, whereas body length growth will tend to continue, thereby reducing the condition factor
(Mørkøre and Rørvik, 2001). Good growth conditions may facilitate the onset of maturation in Atlantic
salmon under culture conditions (e.g., Good and Davison, 2016) and signs of early maturation should
therefore be monitored closely in the Andfjord salmon system.

The overall FCR is comparable to or lower than that seen in studies utilising traditional sea cage rearing,
where an increase in FCR is commonly seen towards the end of the year. For the period between early
September until early January, Mørkøre and Rørvik (2001) observed an FCR above 2.0.

The levels of salmon louse (Lepeophtheirus salmonis) observed are negligible. There is, however, a
noticeable level of Caligus elongatus observed, reaching over 0.5 lice per fish on average in September,
albeit low compared with observed levels in a previous study from UK cage farming of Atlantic salmon
(Revie et al, 2002). Caligus elongatus is known to cause less severe damage to the skin of Atlantic
salmon, compared with Lepeophtheirus salmonis, however with some risk of secondary infections and
reduced welfare. Whilst the Andfjord salmon systems seawater intake is at 40 meters depth, the vertical
distribution of Caligus elongatus may exceed this. The swimming capacity of Caligus elongatus is
generally better than that of Lepeophtheirus salmonis, and the parasite can easily move between hosts.
Little is known about the spatial distribution and temporal dynamics of this species (a Nordi et al., 2015).
Chemical treatments, e.g., as administered through feed, is effective against Caligus elongatus
(Sommerset et al., 2022, Hemmingsen et al., 2020).

6 Summary

As stated earlier, knowledge on selecting and applying appropriate, fit for purpose OWI and LABWI
toolboxes for documenting fish welfare and health in new and emerging aquaculture systems is currently
lacking. An aim of the Andfjord Salmon documentation project was therefore to use a wide-ranging and
comprehensive suite of OWls/LABWIs to document the possible effects of the rearing system and its
operations upon the welfare and health of the fish, and Andfjord Salmon have diligently applied and
developed this monitoring toolbox for the first six months of the documentation project.

Overall, the welfare and health of fish in the system for the first six months of the documentation project
has been good in relation to e.g., the Laksvel scoring scheme for input- and outcome-based indicators
for salmon. A wide range of environmental OWls have documented mostly uniform high levels of key
water quality parameters such as oxygen throughout the pool. Water current speeds, whilst low, have
not manifested in poor water quality and seems to be increased by fish presence in the system. Minor
differences in measured water quality parameters were found at different locations in the system but
should not have consequence for fish welfare and should allow for uniform distribution of fish in the
system so far. As water is drawn from depth before being pumped into the system, water temperature
is somewhat more stable than what would be expected at corresponding depths in open cages and is
following seasonal patterns in ambient water temperature. Although the oxygen has been added to the
inlet water the effect of water temperature and increased biomass on dissolved oxygen saturation was
observed during most intensive parts of the production. The low natural water temperatures the fish
have been subjected to over the autumn/early winter have currently not manifested in any welfare or
performance risks, or risks for e.g., winter ulcers.

Mortalities have been very low in the rearing system (ca. 1.6% for the entire documentation period) and
any minor mortality events are closely monitored and remedial actions, where possible to identify and
apply, have been taken. Appetite has generally been good in the first six months of the project and
growth performance and condition factor have been good. Fish have adapted to the rearing system and
are generally utilising the pool in a uniform manner. No fleeing/panic behaviours have been observed
at observation times around high and low water, and where potential fish sensitivity to the ROV lighting
in late October led to short-term panic in some fish and a short-term increase in associated mortalities,
remedial action was taken to ensure this unfortunate occurrence was short-lived and corrected for.

The injury status of fish in the system is being closely monitored during monthly manual audits of high
numbers of fish in relation to the Laksvel OWI scoring system (Nilsson et al., 2022). The injury status of
the fish has generally been good with the majority of injury based OWlIs being scored as absent or mild.
When OWI levels increase this was noted and causes investigated. After smolt transfer there were a
number of fish with poor condition (emaciation state) and scale loss (< 20% of fish sampled) but this
injury status improved 90 days after stocking.

The health status of the fish following smolt transfer has been documented via monthly samples from
kidney, skin, and gills for histological examination to evaluate the integrity and quality of these organs.
Gill health was generally good at all timepoints, whereas skin got a higher score (more damaged) from
the hatchery phase up until 60 days post-transfer to the pool. This skin health score is higher than what
is expected from fish sampled during similar stages in the production cycle. Histological findings in skin
were comparable to the welfare score and included superficial damages such as lost epidermis and
scale loss over the same time points, in the majority of analysed individuals. Based on these findings,
skin morphology and skin health will be followed closer in the coming months. Fish developed
Nephrocalcinosis in the hatchery phase before transfer to the Andfjord pool and we see the artefacts of
this after their transfer to seawater. With regard to pathogen screening, fish from TO-T5 were all
categorized with suspected heart- and skeletal muscle inflammation (HSMI), with positive results for

45
og

Piscine orthoreovirus 1 (PRV-1). At T4 and T5, 3% of sampled fish was positive for the parasite
Paranucleospora theridion. There were negative results for all other pathogen screening.

Whilst only a negligible amount of Lepeophtheirus salmonis has been observed in the system, there is
a noticeable but relatively low abundance of Caligus elongatus. For now, this does not pose a major risk
to fish health or welfare and can easily be remedied by feed administered treatments. Infestation levels
should nevertheless be monitored closely for the remainder of the production cycle.
